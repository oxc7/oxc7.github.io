In 2017, Japan's SME Agency estimated that by 2025 about 2.45 million owners of small and mid-sized companies would be over 70, and that roughly half of them had no successor lined up. 2025 has now passed, and the estimate held up well enough that people stopped calling it a forecast. It became a deal pipeline. Many of the companies coming to market today are not in trouble and are not being sold for strategic reasons. They are profitable businesses whose owner is getting old and whose children went to work somewhere else.

I spend much of my time on companies like these. The AI question inside them is different from the one people usually ask, and I think the common framing gets the order wrong.

## The common framing

It goes like this: these companies run on paper and fax, AI is cheap now, so buy them and automate. Parts of that are true, and I wrote about [how I look for those improvements](/blog/ai-opportunities-no-ai-companies/) in an earlier post. But it treats the company as a set of processes waiting to be digitized. In a succession deal, that misses the thing most at risk.

## What does not transfer at closing

When a founder-owner sells, the buyer gets the factory, the customer list, the contracts, and the staff. What the buyer does not automatically get is the owner's judgment. In a company run by one person for thirty or forty years, a surprising amount of the business lives there. Which customers can absorb a price increase this year. Which supplier tends to ship short in December. Why a loss-making product line is still alive (because one large customer buys it together with everything else). None of this is written down, because it never had to be. The owner was always in the building.

Most succession deals include a transition period in which the former owner stays on as an adviser or chairman, often for a year or two. On paper that period is about relationships: introducing the new management to customers, suppliers, and the bank. In practice it is also the last time the owner's judgment can be recorded. Whatever has not been captured by the time they leave goes with them.

So in a succession deal, my first AI question is not what we can automate. It is what this person knows that nobody has written down, and how much of it we can capture before they go.

## What capture looks like

None of this needs exotic technology. Speech-to-text and a language model have made the expensive part, turning conversation into structured notes, nearly free. The constraint is the owner's time.

**Record the reasoning, not just the decisions.** Sit with the owner while they review quotes, schedules, or credit limits, and ask why. A few hours a week of recorded conversation becomes a searchable record organized by customer, product, and situation.

**Check the stated rules against the data.** Owners describe rules of thumb that their own transaction history sometimes contradicts. Both are useful. Where the two agree, you have a rule you can rely on. Where they disagree, you have a question to ask while the one person who can answer it is still around.

**Build something the next person can consult, before building anything that decides.** The useful first-year tool answers "how did we handle this customer last time, and why?" An automated pricing engine can come later, after the captured judgment has been tested against outcomes.

Many of these owners chose a long-term holder because they care what happens to the company and its people after they leave. Being asked to explain how they think, by someone who plans to keep that thinking alive in the business, lands very differently from being handed a digital transformation plan by a stranger.

That is also why I make the management team part of every decision rather than the audience for one, as I described in the [earlier post](/blog/ai-opportunities-no-ai-companies/). In the first year of a succession deal, the owner and the people around them are the source material.

## What it means for diligence and the deal

Two practical consequences.

First, how much of the business sits in one person's head is a diligence finding. I treat it the same way as [key-person risk in a technology company](/blog/ai-technical-due-diligence/): a company where the owner makes every important call personally is worth less to a buyer who cannot capture that judgment, and potentially more to one who can.

Second, the transition period should be negotiated with capture in mind. How long it lasts, how much of the owner's time it includes, and what they agree to help document are worth settling explicitly in the deal rather than leaving to goodwill after closing. A year of an owner's structured time is one of the most valuable things a succession buyer can secure, and it is usually treated as an afterthought.

## The clock on each deal

The succession wave usually gets described in terms of supply: many companies, motivated sellers, reasonable prices. That part is real. Less discussed is that every individual deal also has a clock on it. The owner's knowledge is the most perishable asset in the company, and it starts to disappear the day the deal closes. The buyers who do well with these businesses will be the ones who treat the handover period as the most important project of the hold.
